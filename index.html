          The Joint Chiefs

          Uniformed leadership · statutory 4-year terms

          The one corner of Washington with a built-in clock. Four-star officers reach the top after roughly 35 years of service and are pushed out by law and by mandatory retirement, so the entire body sits inside a narrow band in its late 50s and early 60s. General Dan Caine has chaired the Joint Chiefs since April 2025. Flag-officer birth dates are not consistently published, so these are close estimates, marked with dashed rings.

          Share of career money that came from PACs, by generation

          The Silent Generation members of this roster have taken five times the PAC share that Millennials have. But read that carefully — see the caveat below, because age is not the only thing separating these groups.

          What kind of money each generation takes

          The mix shifts as sharply as the amount. Corporate PACs supply roughly 45% of committee money to everyone born before 1981 and about a fifth of it to Millennials. Labour runs the other way. The single Gen Z member takes almost no corporate money at all.

          Read this before you use the generation numbers

          This roster is not a random sample. The page deliberately tracks the oldest and youngest members of Congress, so the age gap here is wider than in Congress as a whole. Treat the direction as real and the magnitude as inflated.

          Age and era are tangled together. Older members started raising money when PAC cheques were the only practical way to fund a campaign. Small-dollar online fundraising only became viable around 2004 and dominant after 2018. First-cycle year correlates with PAC share at r = −0.42 on its own, so some of what looks generational is really just the year someone started.

          Seniority attracts PAC money regardless of age. Committee chairs and leadership raise more from organised interests because they hold the gavel, not because of when they were born. Every long-serving member on this page is also a senior one.

          How this was built

          Two kinds of number appear on this page. Chamber-wide figures — medians, averages, counts of members over 80 — come from published analyses of the full body and are labelled with the date they were measured. Individual ages are computed live in your browser from each person's date of birth, so the plots and the finder recalculate every time the page loads.

          The individual list is a sample, not a census. All nine Supreme Court justices and the full serving cabinet are here. For Congress and the governors, it covers leadership plus both ends of the age range — the people who set the record, not all 535. There is no public dataset of verified birth dates for every member that could be reproduced honestly here.

          Estimates are marked. Dashed rings and a “≈” mean the birth date is not reliably published — chiefly the uniformed service chiefs, and a small number of executive officials. Any average that includes an estimate says so.

          Term status is current as of 13 August 2026: eleven Senate retirements, two primary defeats, sixty House retirements and fifteen term-limited governors. Candidate ages come from published birth dates; a nominee is not a winner, so the ledger shows the range a seat can land in, not a prediction.

          Campaign finance is rebuilt from the FEC's own bulk downloads — candidate summaries for every cycle since 1980, itemised committee cheques since 2018. No API key, no intermediary. The trend charts use election cycles only and start at 1990, because senators raise almost nothing between races and the FEC's PAC field is incomplete before then. The current cycle is partial. Dark money, bundling and all state-level fundraising are invisible to this data. A high PAC share says who paid for a campaign; it is not evidence of a vote traded.

          The national baseline is a median age of 39.1 for the United States, per the Census Bureau.
